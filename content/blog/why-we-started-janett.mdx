The science of learning is not a mystery. For over a century, cognitive scientists have been uncovering the principles that govern how people acquire and retain knowledge, and the findings have held up unusually well.

**Retrieval practice** strengthens memory far more than passive review. **Spaced repetition** defeats the forgetting curve. **Interleaving** builds flexible, transferable skill. **Desirable difficulties** — challenges that slow initial learning while improving long-term retention — are mechanisms rather than obstacles.

Teaching has its own accumulated findings. Worked examples before independent problem solving. Feedback delivered while it can still change what the learner does. Explanation that puts the cognitive work on the learner rather than the instructor.

None of this is especially controversial. Almost none of it is built.

## The Problem We Work On

The tools people learn from — textbooks, video courses, flashcard apps, and now a rapidly growing category of AI tutors — are largely designed around convenience and engagement. They optimize for the feeling of productivity rather than the fact of it. Learners still highlight, re-read, and cram, and their software rarely interrupts them.

This is a translation failure more than a knowledge failure. The bottleneck is not information, and at this point it is not capability either. It is that the distance between an established finding and a working system is much larger than it looks, and almost nobody is paid to close it carefully.

That distance is the lab.

## Why Software Is the Right Instrument

Software is the first medium that can act on this science at scale. It can time a review to the hour it matters, vary a problem so that its surface changes while its structure does not, detect a misconception from a pattern of errors, and hold a conversation about it. Language models widen that range considerably — conversational instruction, which used to require a human tutor, is now cheap.

But capability is not evidence. A system that *can* do these things is not a system that is *known* to help. Most of what is currently being built has never been tested against an outcome that survives a delay, and the field's dominant metrics — engagement, completion, time on task — can all be improved without teaching anyone anything.

So software is both halves of our work. It is the object of study, because it is what people actually learn from now. And it is the apparatus: the only practical way to run instructional experiments at a precision where the effects become legible.

## How We Intend to Work

A few commitments, stated early so they can be held against us:

1. **We build instruments, not products.** A system exists here so that a question can be asked precisely, under conditions we control.

2. **We test against durable outcomes.** Retention weeks after the session ends. Transfer to problems that do not resemble the ones practiced. Anything measurable in a single sitting is a proxy, and we will name it as one.

3. **We publish what fails.** A negative result about a technique that thousands of tools already ship is worth more than another demonstration that something can be built.

4. **We release the artifacts.** Code, task materials, and data where consent allows, so that what we claim can be checked rather than believed.

## What's Next

We are small and intend to stay focused. The first work is on sequencing — what a learner should see next and why — because it is the decision learning software makes most often and justifies least.

Learning is the skill that makes other skills possible. Improving how it happens is among the highest-leverage problems available, and it is currently being addressed mostly by intuition. We would like to change the ratio.

We will share what we find, including the parts that do not work.
